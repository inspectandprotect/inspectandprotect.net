Renting in Florida? Why a Move-In Inspection Protects You (and Your Deposit)

For houses, duplexes, condos and townhouses: what can go wrong when you move in blind, how a report protects your deposit, and what we check.

Most renters move in blind

When you buy a home, an inspection is a normal part of the process. When you rent, most people do a quick walk-through, sign the lease and get the keys. You see the paint, the floors and the kitchen. You don't see the slow leak under the sink, the outlet that isn't grounded, the AC that's struggling, or the moisture behind the wall.

Then something breaks a few months in, and the question becomes: was it already like that, or did you cause it? Without proof of the condition on day one, it's your word against your landlord's.

For every type of rental

A move-in inspection makes sense whatever you're renting in Florida:

Single-family homes: you're often responsible for more of the upkeep, so it's important to know the condition of the roof, AC, water heater, plumbing and electrical before you take it on.

Duplexes: you share walls, and sometimes plumbing or a roof, with your neighbor. A leak or problem that starts on their side can show up on yours, so a record of your unit's condition protects you.

Condos: the association usually maintains the building, while the owner, your landlord, is responsible for the inside of the unit. We document the unit itself: walls, ceilings, floors, windows, electrical, plumbing, the water heater and the AC. That way you know what's working, and you can tell whether a stain on the ceiling came from the unit above, not from you.

Townhouses: you may share walls and a roof line with your neighbors, and the association may handle some exterior items. We check everything inside your unit plus the parts of the exterior and roof we can safely see and reach.

What can go wrong

You get blamed for damage that was already there. A cracked tile, a stained ceiling, a broken window latch or a damaged cabinet can end up deducted from your security deposit when you move out.

You end up paying for repairs. If your lease makes you responsible for certain repairs, a problem that existed before you moved in can quietly become yours to fix.

Your health and safety are at risk. Missing or dead smoke detectors, unsafe wiring, a water heater with no working relief valve, or hidden mold can affect you and your family every day you live there.

Your belongings are at risk. A roof leak, a plumbing leak or a clogged AC drain line can ruin furniture, electronics and clothing.

Your bills go up. An AC unit that's low on performance, leaky ducts, or windows and doors that don't seal can mean higher electric bills all summer.

Know the rules in Florida

Under Florida Statute 83.51, Florida landlords must follow local building, housing and health codes, keep the structure in good repair and keep the plumbing in reasonable working condition. But the law also says the landlord isn't responsible for conditions caused by the tenant. That's why the condition at move-in matters so much.

The rules depend on the type of home. In a single-family home or duplex, the landlord must install working smoke detectors at the start of the tenancy unless the lease says otherwise, and some repair duties can be shifted to the tenant in writing. In other rentals, such as apartments and condo units, the landlord must also provide for pest extermination, working locks and keys, garbage removal, heat, running water and hot water, unless the lease says otherwise in writing.

Under Florida Statute 83.49, when your lease ends the landlord has 15 days to return your deposit if they aren't making a claim against it. If they are, they have 30 days to send you written notice of the amount and the reason, and you have 15 days after you receive it to object in writing. If you have a dated report with photos from move-in day, your objection is backed by evidence instead of memory.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Read your lease carefully, and talk to an attorney if you're in a dispute with your landlord.

How a move-in inspection protects you

A dated record of the condition. You get a written report with photos showing exactly how the home looked and worked the day you moved in.

Leverage before you sign. If the inspection finds problems, you can ask the landlord to fix them before you move in, or put them in writing as existing conditions so they're never charged to you.

Proof at move-out. If the landlord tries to keep part of your deposit for something that was already there, you can show the report.

Peace of mind. You know the electrical, plumbing, AC and safety devices are working before your family moves in.

What we check

Roof and ceilings: signs of active or past leaks, stains and soft spots.

Electrical: outlets tested for grounding and correct wiring, GFCI protection in kitchens, bathrooms and outdoors, the electrical panel and visible wiring.

Plumbing: leaks under sinks, water pressure, drains, toilets and the water heater.

Air conditioning: whether it cools properly, the condition of the unit and the drain line.

Moisture and mold: we use a moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera to find hidden moisture behind walls and under floors. If you're worried about mold, we also offer mold inspection and testing.

Safety: smoke detectors, stairs and railings, and doors and windows that open, close and lock.

Doors, windows, floors, walls and cabinets: existing damage documented with photos.

Signs of pests such as droppings or damaged wood.

When to schedule it

The best time is before you sign the lease, so you can ask for repairs while you still have leverage. The next best time is before you move your belongings in, while the home is empty and every wall, floor and cabinet is easy to see. Let your landlord or property manager know you're having it inspected, and share the report with them so you're both working from the same record.

Renters can also book an inspection before moving out, so you have a second record showing how you're leaving the home.

What you get

You get a detailed report with photos, usually the same day. It's written in plain English so you know what each finding means and whether it's a safety issue, a repair, or something to keep an eye on. You can see what our reports look like in our sample reports.

A few hundred dollars up front can protect a security deposit worth much more, and it can keep you and your family out of a home with problems you didn't know about.
